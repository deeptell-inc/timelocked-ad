# Does hiding the post-processing from a memory-limited eavesdropper raise the noise tolerance of QKD? Timelocked advantage distillation and the 20% barrier

## Abstract

Advantage distillation (AD) raises the finite-key threshold quantum bit error rate (QBER) of decoy-state BB84 from 9.5% to 17.3% [T25]; asymptotically two-way post-processing tolerates 20% (BB84) and 27.6% (six-state). Bae and Acín showed that an adversary who stores her probes until the AD parities are announced attains these limits, and that one forced by a delay to measure beforehand is held off until the 25% entanglement limit. We ask whether such a delay, realised as a quantum computational timelock (a plain delay or a post-quantum cipher) hiding the parities and the reconciliation syndrome for longer than the adversary's quantum memory survives, raises the finite-key threshold. Reusing the decoy-state statistics of Ref. [T25] and replacing only the adversary term, evaluated for the symmetric collective attack, we find that the answer depends on how much memory she has, not on whether it is shorter than the delay. A per-probe adversary does gain: at $10^{14}$ pulses the threshold rises from 17.3% to 20.7–20.9%, with 25% as asymptote. But an adversary who holds the $b$ probes of one block for the microseconds they take to arrive and measures them jointly, still before the parities are known, already attains the adaptive Helstrom value: we prove, by an explicit measurement, that the best non-adaptive joint measurement equals the adaptive one for every block size, so that the asymptote is 20% and the finite-key threshold returns to 17.3–18.4%. Hiding the basis as well does not help BB84, and for six-state the memoryless gain known without AD shrinks by an order of magnitude at two-bit blocks. Timelocking the post-processing buys nothing beyond unconditional security unless the adversary's memory is shorter than a block.

## 1. Introduction

Quantum key distribution promises keys whose secrecy does not rest on computational assumptions, but the promise is only redeemed when the channel is quiet enough. Every QKD protocol has a maximum QBER above which no key can be certified, and for the most deployed protocol, decoy-state BB84 with one-way post-processing, that maximum is close to 9–11% depending on the source model [T25, Lim14]. Daylight free-space links, fibres shared with bright classical traffic, and long satellite passes routinely approach or exceed this level, so raising the threshold without changing the optics is one of the few remaining software levers on QKD range.

Advantage distillation is the classical lever. Introduced by Maurer for classical key agreement [Maurer93] and brought into QKD by Gottesman and Lo [GL03], it post-selects blocks of raw bits whose announced parities agree, so that the surviving bits are far better correlated between Alice and Bob than between Alice and Eve. Chau showed that BB84 tolerates 20.0% and six-state QKD 27.6% asymptotically with iterated two-way post-processing [Chau02]; Acín and co-workers proved these values tight for the repetition-code protocol under collective attacks, with an explicit attack in which Eve stores her probes until the parities are public and then discriminates two product states [ABBMM06, BA07]. A finite-key treatment for sending-or-not-sending twin-field QKD was given by Jiang et al. [Jiang21], sharper finite-blocklength entropy bounds for two-way distillation have recently been obtained from hypothesis testing [BMB26], and for decoy-state BB84 the first complete finite-key analysis is that of Treplin, Kleinpaß and Orsucci [T25], who proved that a $b$-bit repetition-code AD step raises the finite-key threshold from 9.5% to 17.3% at $N=10^{14}$ pulses. Their formula bounds Eve's smooth min-entropy about the distilled bits through an entropic uncertainty relation on the post-selected state, and it allows the adversary to hold a purification of everything until the last message of the protocol has been announced.

A different line of work asks what changes if the adversary cannot wait. Bae and Acín already observed that a delay between distribution and reconciliation, longer than the decoherence time of Eve's memory, forces her to measure before the parities are announced, and that for the parity-announcing form of AD this restores the entanglement limit of 25% for BB84 as the security condition, whereas for the form in which Alice announces positions of equal bits it restores nothing [BA07]. In the cryptographic literature the same assumption is the bounded- and noisy-storage model [DFSS05, WST08], under which two-party primitives become possible. Watanabe, Matsumoto and Uyematsu encrypted the parities of a two-way pre-processing step with a pre-shared one-time pad and obtained a higher net key rate in the Shor–Preskill framework [WMU06]. Pastushenko and Kronberg encrypted the error-correction data and argued that, if Eve's quantum memory decoheres before she learns the codeword set, her information is bounded by the additive accessible information rather than by the Holevo quantity [PK23]; they did not claim composable security. Novak then proved, in the non-asymptotic framework of Tomamichel and Leverrier [TL17], that one-time-pad protection of one-way error-correction data drawn from the same session leaves the key rate exactly unchanged at fixed security level [Novak25], and Fung, Ma, Chau and Cai had shown that delaying privacy amplification leaves security and key rate unchanged [FMCC12]. Neither encryption nor delay by itself buys anything; whatever gain exists must come from an assumption about the adversary's memory. Vyas and Alléaume made that assumption explicit as the quantum computational hybrid model, in which a computationally secure cipher cannot be broken before a time $t_{\rm comp}$ and any quantum memory decoheres within $t_{\rm coh}<t_{\rm comp}$; their MUB-QCT protocol timelocks the basis information of a high-dimensional encoding and achieves everlasting security at rates beyond QKD [VA20, MBBA25].

This paper asks whether the same assumption helps the most deployed protocol when the hardware is left untouched and only the AD and reconciliation messages are timelocked, and it answers the question raised by the remark of Ref. [BA07]: what exactly must be assumed about the adversary's memory for the parity-announcing form of AD to beat 20%? Under the timelock the adversary must measure before she learns the parities, so her side information is classical and non-adaptive, and the problem becomes one of state discrimination with post-measurement information [BWW08, GW10]. Our contributions are: (i) a threat model that states precisely which announcement delays make the adversary non-adaptive, and the observation that a post-quantum cipher serves only to remove the latency of a plain delay; (ii) a finite-key secure key length that reuses the decoy-state statistics of Ref. [T25] and replaces only the adversary term, implemented numerically and validated by reproducing all four published thresholds of that work; (iii) the result that an adversary who measures each probe separately, the "individual attack" of Ref. [BA07], gains in the finite-key regime (the threshold at $N=10^{14}$ rises to 20.7–20.9% with the 25% limit of Gisin and Wolf [GW99] as asymptote); (iv) the result that an adversary who may hold the $b$ probes of one block, a time of microseconds, and measure them jointly before the parities are announced, loses nothing by the delay: we prove (Theorem 1) that the best non-adaptive joint measurement equals the adaptive one for every $b$, with an explicit basis, so that the asymptote is the 20% of Refs. [Chau02, BA07], and the finite-key threshold returns to that of Ref. [T25], so that the per-probe reading of the remark of Ref. [BA07] does not survive a memory of block length; and (v) the result that hiding the basis announcement as well, which helps a memoryless adversary without AD for the six-state protocol [BAL11], does not compound with AD. We also record a condition on the domain of validity of the key-length formula of Ref. [T25] that any implementation must enforce.

## 2. Model and assumptions

**A1 (quantum computational timelock).** A cipher $E_k$ shared by Alice and Bob cannot be broken by the adversary before time $t_{\rm comp}$ after its use; any quantum memory available to the adversary decoheres within $t_{\rm coh}<t_{\rm comp}$ [VA20]. The technology assessment in Ref. [VA20] places $t_{\rm coh}$ of optical quantum memories below one second, whereas $t_{\rm comp}$ for a post-quantum cipher is measured in years; the assumption is only required to hold during the protocol run, which is what makes the resulting key everlasting.

**A2 (announcement delay).** Let $t_{\rm rx}$ be the time at which the adversary intercepts a pulse and $t_m$ the time at which a post-processing message $m$ is sent. Message $m$ is hidden from the adversary for a delay $\Delta_m$, realised either as a plain delay $\tau_{\rm ann}$ before publication or by encryption under $E_k$ (then $\Delta_m=t_{\rm comp}-t_m$). The requirement is
$$\Delta_m > t_{\rm coh} - (t_m - t_{\rm rx}).$$
In tier V-A only the AD parities $c_A, c_B$ and the reconciliation syndrome are delayed and the basis announcement is immediate; in tier V-B the basis announcement is delayed as well. For a protocol that keys only from $Z$-basis events, as Protocol 1 of Ref. [T25] does, V-B is no stronger than V-A: the adversary optimises her measurement for the $Z$ events she cares about, and the hidden basis does not change her task. We analyse V-A and return to protocols that key from both bases in Section 3.

**A3 (physical layer).** As in Ref. [T25]: phase-randomised coherent states with three intensities $\mu_1>\mu_2+\mu_3$, $\mu_3\approx 0$, active basis choice, threshold detectors with random assignment of double clicks, and an authenticated classical channel.

**A4 (adversary).** Each pulse interacts with an independent ancilla through the symmetric unitary of Refs. [FGGNP97, GW99] (a collective attack). Before $t_{\rm coh}$ the adversary may apply any measurement to her ancillas; after it, her side information is classical. By A2 the measurement does not depend on the delayed messages. We consider two classes: **individual**, in which each ancilla is measured separately after the basis announcement, which is the attack class called individual in Ref. [BA07] and requires $t_{\rm coh}$ shorter than the pulse spacing; and **joint**, in which the $b$ ancillas of an AD block are measured together, still before the parities are known, which requires only that $t_{\rm coh}$ exceed the duration of a block (microseconds at gigahertz clock rates) and is the natural reading of A1. The reduction from coherent to collective attacks and the question of joint measurements across blocks are discussed in Section 7.

**A5 (statistics).** Finite-size fluctuations of the decoy estimates, the block post-selection and the error-rate extrapolation are handled exactly as in Ref. [T25]; we also verify the headline numbers with the virtual-i.i.d. reduction of Ref. [Jiang21].

The role of the cipher follows from A2. A plain delay $\tau_{\rm ann}>t_{\rm coh}$ is sufficient and needs no cryptography, at the price of a key-delivery latency of $\tau_{\rm ann}$. Encryption under a post-quantum cipher removes that latency while keeping the adversary's delay at $t_{\rm comp}$. The cipher compresses latency; it does not create the gain, which is the reading of Ref. [PK23] that survives the equivalence theorem of Ref. [Novak25].

## 3. The adversary's information on a distilled bit

Under A1 and A2 the adversary's optimal strategy is an immediate measurement followed by classical decoding once the delayed messages become available (Ref. [VA20], Proposition 1). In the symmetric attack each pulse interacts as
$$U|\!\uparrow\rangle|0\rangle=\sqrt{F}|\!\uparrow\rangle\psi_\uparrow+\sqrt{D}|\!\downarrow\rangle\varphi_\uparrow,\qquad U|\!\downarrow\rangle|0\rangle=\sqrt{F}|\!\downarrow\rangle\psi_\downarrow+\sqrt{D}|\!\uparrow\rangle\varphi_\downarrow,$$
with disturbance $D$ equal to the single-photon QBER and $F=(1+c_\varphi)/(2-c_\psi+c_\varphi)$, $c_\psi=\langle\psi_\uparrow|\psi_\downarrow\rangle$, $c_\varphi=\langle\varphi_\uparrow|\varphi_\downarrow\rangle$ [GW99]. The $\psi$ and $\varphi$ subspaces are orthogonal, so the adversary can always learn whether a bit was disturbed ($Z_1=X\oplus Y$) without affecting anything else. After a $b$-bit block is accepted and both parity strings are announced, she knows whether the block was accepted with all bits correct (probability $p_u=(1-D)^b/p_a$, $p_a=(1-D)^b+D^b$) or all bits wrong, and in the first case her $b$ ancillas are in the product state $\psi_{a_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{a_b}$, where the parities fix all $a_i$ relative to the distilled bit $a_1$.

**Individual measurement.** This is the per-probe adversary of Ref. [BA07], reported here as a reference point rather than as a result; we restrict her to the Helstrom measurement of each ancilla followed by optimal decoding, which is the model of Ref. [GW99]. Measuring each ancilla by the Helstrom measurement gives a guess of each bit that is correct with probability $\delta_0=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sqrt{1-c_\psi^2}$ on undisturbed bits and $\delta_1=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sqrt{1-c_\varphi^2}$ on disturbed ones; with the parities the guesses become $b$ votes on $a_1$. The tally $k$ of votes for 0 is a sufficient statistic, the majority vote is its maximum-likelihood decision with error $\gamma_b(\delta)$, and the adversary's information on the distilled bit is
$$I_E^{\rm ind}(D,b)=\max_{c_\varphi}\Big[p_u\,J_b(\delta_0)+(1-p_u)\,J_b(\delta_1)\Big],\qquad J_b(\delta)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{b}\Pr(k)\,h\big(\Pr(a_1=0\,|\,k)\big),$$
with $\Pr(a_1=0|k)=\delta^k(1-\delta)^{b-k}/[\delta^k(1-\delta)^{b-k}+(1-\delta)^k\delta^{b-k}]$. We stress that $J_b(\delta)\ge 1-h(\gamma_b(\delta))$: the Fano expression built from the majority-vote error, which is what the asymptotic analysis of Ref. [GW99] needs, is only a lower bound on the adversary's information and must not be used in a key-length formula. For every $b\ge2$ the maximum is at $c_\varphi=1$, $\delta_0=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sqrt{1-(3-2/(1-D))^2}$. This is the incoherent-attack model of Ref. [GW99]; its Theorem 1 states that key agreement by AD is possible iff $D/(1-D)<2\sqrt{\delta_0(1-\delta_0)}=|c_\psi|$, which for the 4-state protocol is $D<1/4$, and it is the individual-attack security condition for the parity-announcing protocol in Ref. [BA07].

**Joint measurement.** If the adversary may measure the $b$ ancillas together, but still before the parities are announced, her task is to discriminate the $2^b$ product states $\psi_{a_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{a_b}$ with the post-measurement information $c$, which partitions them into $2^{b-1}$ pairs $\{\psi_a,\psi_{\bar a}\}$ [BWW08, GW10]. Her success probability is the value of the semidefinite programme
$$P^{\rm joint}_b=\max_{\{M_f\}}\ 2^{-b}\sum_{a}\mathrm{Tr}\big[\rho_a E_a\big],\qquad E_a=\sum_{f:\,f(c(a))=a_1}M_f,\quad M_f\ge0,\quad \sum_f M_f=\mathbb 1,$$
over POVMs indexed by the functions $f:\{0,1\}^{b-1}\to\{0,1\}$ that assign a guess to every possible parity string. It is upper-bounded by the adaptive value $P^{\rm ad}_b=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sqrt{1-c_\psi^{2b}}$, the Helstrom probability for the two product states of a known pair, since the single-copy overlap $c_\psi$ becomes $c_\psi^{b}$ for $b$ copies; this is the attack of Refs. [ABBMM06, BA07], which an adversary with memory implements after the announcement by an adaptive sequence of single-copy measurements [ABBMM05]. The bound is attained for every $b$:

**Theorem 1.** *Let $\psi_0,\psi_1$ be pure states with real overlap $c$, and write $\Psi_a=\psi_{a_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{a_b}$. In a basis with $\psi_1=Z\psi_0$ let $\Psi=\psi_0^{\otimes b}$, $G=Z^{\otimes b}$, $u\propto(1+G)\Psi$, $v\propto(1-G)\Psi$ normalised, and $g_\pm=(u\pm v)/\sqrt2$. For $y'\in\{0,1\}^{b-1}$ put $\tilde y=(1\oplus|y'|,\,y')$, let $L$ be the prefix-parity map $(Ly')_j=\bigoplus_{i\le j}y'_i$, and $P_{y'}=X^{\tilde y}Z^{(0,Ly')}$. Then $\{P_{y'}g_+,P_{y'}g_-\}_{y'}$ is an orthonormal basis, and the projective measurement in it, read after the parities with the rule "$a_1=0$ iff the outcome sign is $(-1)^{y'\cdot(Ly'\oplus c)}$", guesses $a_1$ with probability $P^{\rm ad}_b=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\sqrt{1-c^{2b}}$ for every parity class $c$.*

The proof (Appendix A) is short: the basis is a Pauli orbit of the class-0 Helstrom pair, orthonormality follows from $\langle\psi_0|XZ|\psi_0\rangle=0$ for real states together with a property of the prefix-parity map, and the Helstrom projector of every class is a sum of basis projectors because $X^{\tilde y}$ anticommutes with $G$ and commutes with the class translations $Z^{(0,c)}$ up to a sign. In the picture in which the probes carry bit flips, the outcome $(\tilde y,s)$ is a guess of the odd-weight parity $\tilde y\cdot a$, which distinguishes $a$ from $\bar a$ for every class; the post-measurement information only tells the adversary how to translate that parity into $a_1$. Independently, solving the semidefinite programme for $b=2,3,4$ and $D\in[0.05,0.24]$ gives $P^{\rm joint}_b=P^{\rm ad}_b$ to within $10^{-7}$ (Table 2), and the construction of Theorem 1 reproduces $P^{\rm ad}_b$ to $10^{-14}$ for $b\le9$ in our implementation. Hiding the parities therefore costs the adversary nothing. For the adversary's Shannon information we use the fact that for two equiprobable pure states the Helstrom measurement also maximises the mutual information [Levitin95, Fuchs96], so that $I_E^{\rm joint}(D,b)=\max_{c_\varphi}[p_u(1-h(1-P^{\rm ad}_b(c_\psi)))+(1-p_u)(1-h(1-P^{\rm ad}_b(c_\varphi)))]$ bounds the accessible information of any within-block strategy, adaptive or not; the guessing probability $P^{\rm ad}_b$ bounds the min-entropy version without further assumptions.

The consequence for the asymptotic threshold is immediate. Bob's error on an accepted block is $D^b/p_a\approx(D/(1-D))^b$. The adversary's error is $\approx|c_\psi|^b$ for individual measurement and $\approx c_\psi^{2b}/4$ for joint measurement. Key agreement by the repetition code requires the adversary's error to decay more slowly than Bob's, i.e. $|c_\psi|>D/(1-D)$ in the first case and $c_\psi^2>D/(1-D)$ in the second. With $c_\psi=(1-3D)/(1-D)$ at $c_\varphi=1$ these conditions read $D<1/4$ and $10D^2-7D+1>0$, i.e. $D<1/5$. The second condition is the security condition of Ref. [BA07] (their Eq. (68) with $d=2$), which that work derived for an adversary who measures after the announcement; Theorem 1 shows that the same condition governs an adversary who measures before it. The same algebra for the six-state protocol, whose symmetric attack has $c_\varphi=0$ and hence $c_\psi=(1-2D)/(1-D)$, gives $5D^2-5D+1>0$, i.e. $D<(5-\sqrt5)/10=27.6\%$ [Chau02, ABBMM06]. The 25% limit of Ref. [GW99] is thus the limit for an adversary whose memory is shorter than the spacing between the pulses of a block, not for one whose memory is shorter than the announcement delay.

**Hiding the basis as well.** A protocol that keys from both bases and delays the sifting announcement beyond $t_{\rm coh}$ confronts the adversary with the basis as additional post-measurement information. Without advantage distillation ($b=1$) this is the memoryless-adversary setting solved by Bocquet, Alléaume and Leverrier [BAL11], who optimised Eve's interaction and POVM and found thresholds of 15.4% for BB84 (against 14.6% with memory), 17.6% for SARG04 and 20.4% for six-state (against 15.6%); the gain is confined to protocols with more than two bases, in line with the result of Ref. [BWW08] that two bases admit a common optimal measurement. We extended the semidefinite programme to hidden bases and parities, with Eve's probe states conditioned on Bob's outcome and the accepted-block mixture of all-correct and all-flipped blocks. For BB84 the worst-case attack ($c_\varphi=1$) gives $P^{\rm joint}=P^{\rm ad}$ at $b=1$ and $b=2$ for every $D$; the gap that exists at $c_\varphi=0$ is irrelevant because the adversary chooses $c_\varphi$. For six-state at $b=1$ we reproduce the qualitative gain of Ref. [BAL11] (e.g. $P^{\rm joint}=0.750$ against $P^{\rm ad}=0.833$ at $D=0.167$). But as soon as blocks of two bits are formed the gain collapses: for blocks whose two pulses share an unknown basis, $P^{\rm joint}=P^{\rm ad}$ to $10^{-6}$ (0.888644 against 0.888642 at $D=0.167$, verified with two solvers). For the realistic blocks with independently chosen hidden bases, where the full programme has $2^{18}$ elements, we bracket the adversary's success: the relaxation in which only the bases are hidden and the parities are known gives $P^{\rm joint}\le0.8878$ at $D=0.167$ and $\le0.9556$ at $D=0.259$, and an explicit 16-outcome projective measurement with both hidden, found by first-order optimisation and therefore possibly short of the true optimum, achieves $P\ge0.8826$ and $0.9395$, against $P^{\rm ad}=0.8886$ and $0.9583$. Hiding both therefore costs the adversary between 0.0008 and 0.006 in guessing probability at $D=0.167$ and between 0.003 and 0.019 at $D=0.259$, already at $b=2$, against 0.083 at $b=1$ and $D=0.167$; in terms of her error probability the ratio to the adaptive value falls from 1.5 at $b=1$ to at most 1.05 at $b=2$ and at most 1.04 at $b=3$ at $D=0.167$; at $D=0.259$, close to the six-state limit, our achievable bounds are looser and only give 1.46 and 1.43 at $b=2,3$, while the relaxation shows that the true ratio at $b=2$ is at least 1.06. The construction of Theorem 1 does not extend to hidden bases, because the class translations are then the basis-change unitaries on the adversary's probe rather than Pauli operators, which is why a residual gain survives at finite $b$. Hiding the basis and hiding the parities therefore do not compound: the one-way memoryless gain of Ref. [BAL11] (20.4% for six-state) is dominated by advantage distillation without any assumption (27.6%), and adding the timelock to advantage distillation is worth at most 0.03 bits of min-entropy per distilled bit at $b=2$. The threshold is set by the error exponents of Bob and of the adversary, so a bounded error ratio leaves the 27.6% limit unchanged; the trend from $b=1$ to $b=3$ is evidence for this, not a proof, and we leave the exponent question open.

## 4. Finite-key secure key length

The protocol is Protocol 1 of Ref. [T25] with two modifications: the parity strings $c_A,c_B$ and the reconciliation syndrome are sent under $E_k$ (or after $\tau_{\rm ann}$), and privacy amplification uses the bound below. Let $S^-_{\bar K}$ be the lower bound on the number of accepted blocks consisting only of single-photon events, $\phi_Z^+$ the upper bound on the single-photon $Z$-basis error rate, $N_{\bar K}$ and $\Phi_{\bar K}$ the expected number and error rate of the distilled bits, and $\lambda_{\rm IR}=f N_{\bar K} h(\Phi_{\bar K})$ the reconciliation leakage, all as defined in Eqs. (12)–(20) of Ref. [T25]. Then
$$\ell_{\rm QCT}\le S^-_{\bar K}\big[1-I_E(\phi_Z^+,b)\big]-\Delta_{\rm AEP}(S^-_{\bar K},\epsilon)-\lambda_{\rm IR}-\log_2\frac{2}{\epsilon_{\rm cor}}-4\log_2\frac{2^{7/4}}{\epsilon_{\rm sec}-\epsilon_{\rm sec}^2/8},$$
with $I_E$ either $I_E^{\rm ind}$ or $I_E^{\rm joint}$, where $\Delta_{\rm AEP}=4\log_2(2+\sqrt2)\sqrt{S\log_2(2/\epsilon^2)}$ is the finite-size correction of the asymptotic equipartition property (Theorem 9 of Ref. [TCR09], with $\eta\le 2+\sqrt2$ for a binary variable and classical side information, valid for $S\ge\tfrac85\log_2(2/\epsilon^2)$), evaluated with smoothing parameter $\epsilon=\epsilon_{\rm sec}/4$, and the last two terms are the correctness and leftover-hash terms of Ref. [T25]. We keep the parameter-estimation and hashing budget of Ref. [T25] unchanged and charge the smoothing to an additional $\epsilon_{\rm sec}/4$, so the total secrecy parameter of the stated numbers is $5\epsilon_{\rm sec}/4$. A more conservative variant replaces $S^-_{\bar K}[1-I_E]-\Delta_{\rm AEP}$ by $-S^-_{\bar K}\log_2 p_{\rm guess}$ with $p_{\rm guess}$ the adversary's per-block guessing probability ($1-\gamma_b$ or $P^{\rm ad}_b$, mixed over the two block types), which needs no equipartition step. The use of the upper bound $\phi_Z^+$ in $I_E$ is conservative because $I_E$ increases with $D$ in both models.

Two remarks delimit what this formula is. First, the adversary term is evaluated for the symmetric collective attack family of Section 3, so the key lengths are a model calculation, not a security proof against all timelock-restricted adversaries: they are upper bounds on what any proof within the model could certify. This is the right object for a negative result, since a key length that is already no larger than the unconditional one for this family cannot become larger for a wider one. A further distinction matters. Because $P^{\rm ad}_b$ upper-bounds every within-block strategy, the joint-model key lengths are what the model certifies against any such adversary in the family; that the certified value is also attained, so that no better analysis within the model can exceed it, is the content of Theorem 1. Second, if A2 fails, the adversary may hold her ancillas until the parities are public and the entropic-uncertainty term of Ref. [T25] must be restored; the formula then coincides with Eq. (93) of that work because every other term is shared. The key length vanishes for $\phi_Z^+\ge1/4$ by construction. Within the model, once the adversary's side information is classical the leftover hash lemma against classical side information applies, the accessible-information criterion of Ref. [VA20] and the trace-distance criterion used here coincide, and the security parameter is $\epsilon_{\rm sec}$ conditional on A1 holding during the run; the key is everlasting in the sense of Ref. [VA20] because A1 is needed only then.

## 5. Numerical results

## 6. Implementation considerations

The AD stage adds $b-1$ exclusive-or operations and one comparison per block, fewer than one operation per raw bit, against the several hundred operations per bit of belief-propagation LDPC decoding; hardware key-distillation engines [Walenta14, Constantin17, Cheng25] therefore remain decoder-limited after an AD stage is inserted, whether or not the announcements are delayed. The timelock itself is a buffer of depth $\tau_{\rm ann}$ or an authenticated-encryption wrapper under a post-quantum key-encapsulation mechanism. Given Section 5, neither is a cost worth paying for a $Z$-keyed protocol.

## 7. Discussion

The negative result rests on three steps whose scope should be stated. First, the attack family is the symmetric collective attack of Refs. [FGGNP97, GW99]. For the negative conclusion this is enough: an adversary drawn from a restricted family who already matches the unconditional threshold cannot be beaten by widening the family. For the finite-key numbers it means that they are model calculations in the sense of Section 4. The reduction of coherent attacks to collective ones for a permutation-invariant protocol is given in Appendix B: the post-selection technique of Christandl, König and Renner [CKR09] carries over to the timelock-restricted adversary because the post-selection map acts on her purification before her measurement, the multi-photon rounds are tagged as in Ref. [T25], and a Pauli twirl reduces collective attacks to Bell-diagonal ones [KGR05]. What remains open is the re-maximisation of the adversary term over Bell-diagonal attacks with unequal $X$ and $Z$ error rates; the negative conclusion does not need it, since the adversary is free to choose the symmetric attack. Second, joint measurements across blocks are not covered by the semidefinite programme; since the result is already a collapse of the gain, a cross-block advantage for the adversary could only strengthen it. Third, Theorem 1 settles the within-block question for every block size; the compatibility of the class-wise Helstrom measurements that it establishes is an instance of the questions studied in Ref. [GW10], and the proof identifies the Pauli structure of the class translations as the reason.

The relation to Ref. [BA07] deserves to be made precise. That work distinguishes two forms of AD: CAD1, in which Alice announces the positions of $b$ equal bits, and CAD2, the parity-announcing form used here and in Ref. [T25]. Against an adversary with memory the two are equivalent and both are secure exactly up to 20% for BB84. Against an adversary forced to measure before the announcement, Ref. [BA07] notes that CAD1 is still broken at 20%, because Eve knows in advance that the block carries $b$ copies of the same bit and an adaptive sequence of single-copy measurements discriminates the two product states optimally [ABBMM05], whereas for CAD2 the security condition becomes the entanglement condition $D<1/4$. The present work shows that this last statement holds only for an adversary who measures each probe before the next arrives. If she can hold a block, the parity-conditioned discrimination problems of CAD2 are compatible, and CAD2 is broken at 20% as well. Since the pulses of a block are separated by nanoseconds and the announcement by at least a round trip, a model in which the adversary's memory is shorter than the former but her computation unlimited is not one that a delay or a cipher can enforce; the quantum computational timelock of Ref. [VA20] enforces exactly the weaker condition, and under it the two forms of AD are equivalent again.

Two observations about the reference formula arose in the course of the work. The bound on the logical phase-error rate of the distilled bits, Eq. (19) of Ref. [T25], is proven monotone in the single-photon error rates only on the domain $\phi_X+\phi_Z<1/2$ (Appendix B of that work), because blocks accepted with all $b$ bits flipped carry no phase error: at $\phi_X=0.3$ and $b=7$ the expression falls from 0.499 to 0.25 as $\phi_Z$ rises from 0.3 to 0.5. An unconstrained optimisation of the protocol parameters leaves the domain by driving the estimated single-photon $Z$ error toward one half through a vanishing test set, and then certifies key at QBERs above the asymptotic limit. Any implementation must enforce $\phi_X^++\phi_Z^+<1/2$ as an acceptance condition; we do so, and all four thresholds of Ref. [T25] lie inside the domain. Separately, the Fano expression $1-h(\gamma_b)$ built from the majority-vote error, which suffices for the asymptotic threshold of Ref. [GW99], underestimates a per-probe adversary's Shannon information and must be replaced by the tally information $J_b$ of Section 3 in any finite-key use; the difference is 1.0 percentage points at $N=10^{14}$, $b=9$.

What, then, can a timelock do for a BB84-type protocol? Hiding the parities does not help because the parity-conditioned discrimination problems are compatible. Hiding the basis does not help BB84 at all, and helps six-state only without advantage distillation, where the memoryless-adversary threshold of Ref. [BAL11] applies; a timelock of delay $\tau_{\rm ann}>t_{\rm coh}$ is in fact the operational way to realise the memoryless assumption of that work. Once advantage distillation is used, the block gives the adversary a multi-copy discrimination problem in which the basis-conditioned optimal measurements become compatible again, and the unconditional two-way limits of 20% and 27.6% take over. MUB-QCT [VA20] escapes this because its $d+1$ bases in dimension $d$ do not admit a common measurement and because it does not rely on a parity-announcing post-processing step; the lesson of the present work is that for qubit protocols the basis is the only lever a timelock has, and advantage distillation removes it.

## 8. Conclusion

Keeping the parity and syndrome announcements of advantage-distillation post-processing hidden from an eavesdropper for longer than her quantum memory survives raises the finite-key QBER threshold of decoy-state BB84 only if she is restricted to measuring her probes one at a time, before the next pulse of the block arrives; then 17.3% becomes 20.7–20.9% at $10^{14}$ pulses, with the 25% entanglement limit as asymptote, which is the finite-key form of a remark of Bae and Acín. If she may hold the probes of one block and measure them jointly, even without adaptivity, she loses nothing by the delay (Theorem 1): the asymptotic threshold is 20%, the finite-key threshold returns to 17.3–18.4%, and unconditional security already achieves essentially the same. Hiding the basis as well, which does help a memoryless adversary without advantage distillation for the six-state protocol, does not compound with advantage distillation: two-bit blocks with hidden, independently chosen bases already cut the adversary's loss by an order of magnitude. A quantum computational timelock enforces a memory shorter than the announcement delay, not shorter than a block, and for qubit protocols with advantage distillation that is not enough to beat the unconditional limits. The key-length formula of Treplin et al. must be used within its stated domain, and the per-probe adversary's information must be computed from the vote tally rather than from the majority-vote error.

## Appendix A. Proof of Theorem 1

Choose the qubit basis $|0\rangle\propto\psi_0+\psi_1$, $|1\rangle\propto\psi_0-\psi_1$, so that $\psi_0=\cos\theta|0\rangle+\sin\theta|1\rangle$, $\psi_1=Z\psi_0$ and $c=\cos2\theta$; we assume $|c|<1$. Then $\Psi_a=Z^a\Psi$ with $\Psi=\psi_0^{\otimes b}$, and $\Psi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm G)\Psi$ have norms $\|\Psi_\pm\|^2=\tfrac12(1\pm c^b)$. The class $c$ consists of $Q_c\Psi$ and $GQ_c\Psi$ with $Q_c=Z^{(0,c)}$, and since $Q_c$ commutes with $G$ its Helstrom vectors are $g^{(c)}_\pm=Q_cg_\pm$. For any projector $E$ with $Eg^{(c)}_+=g^{(c)}_+$ and $Eg^{(c)}_-=0$ the success probability on class $c$ is $\tfrac12[\langle Q_c\Psi|E|Q_c\Psi\rangle+\langle GQ_c\Psi|1-E|GQ_c\Psi\rangle]=\tfrac12(\|\Psi_+\|+\|\Psi_-\|)^2=P^{\rm ad}_b$, because $Q_c\Psi=\alpha g^{(c)}_++\beta g^{(c)}_-$ and $GQ_c\Psi=\alpha g^{(c)}_-+\beta g^{(c)}_+$ with $\alpha=(\|\Psi_+\|+\|\Psi_-\|)/\sqrt2$. It therefore suffices to show that the basis of Theorem 1 is orthonormal (Lemma 1) and that for each class the vectors selected by the rule span $g^{(c)}_+$ and are orthogonal to $g^{(c)}_-$ (Lemma 2).

For a product state and real $\psi_0$ one has $\langle\Psi|X^wZ^s|\Psi\rangle=\prod_i\langle\psi_0|X^{w_i}Z^{s_i}|\psi_0\rangle$ with $\langle\psi_0|Z|\psi_0\rangle=c$, $\langle\psi_0|X|\psi_0\rangle=\sin2\theta\neq0$ and $\langle\psi_0|XZ|\psi_0\rangle=0$ ($XZ$ is antisymmetric); hence $\langle\Psi|X^wZ^s|\Psi\rangle=0$ if and only if $w$ and $s$ share a position.

*Lemma 1.* For $y'\neq z'$ and all signs, $\langle P_{y'}g_s|P_{z'}g_{s'}\rangle=0$. Indeed $P_{y'}^\dagger P_{z'}=\pm X^wZ^{(0,r)}$ with $w=\tilde y\oplus\tilde z=(|d|\bmod2,\,d)$, $d=y'\oplus z'\neq0$ and $r=Ld$. As $|w|$ is even, $X^wZ^{(0,r)}$ preserves the eigenspaces of $G$, so the overlap equals $\tfrac12[\langle u|X^wZ^{(0,r)}|u\rangle+ss'\langle v|X^wZ^{(0,r)}|v\rangle]$, and $\langle\Psi_\pm|X^wZ^{(0,r)}|\Psi_\pm\rangle=\tfrac12[\langle\Psi|X^wZ^{(0,r)}|\Psi\rangle\pm\langle\Psi|X^wZ^{(1,\bar r)}|\Psi\rangle]$. Both expectation values vanish, for every $s,s'$, provided (i) $d$ and $Ld$ share a position and (ii) either $|d|$ is odd or $d\not\subseteq Ld$. For the prefix-parity map, $(Ld)_{i_0}=1$ at the first position $i_0$ of $d$, which gives (i); and if $|d|$ is even, $(Ld)_{i_1}=|d|\bmod2=0$ at the last position $i_1$ of $d$, which gives (ii). Within one $y'$, $\langle g_+|g_-\rangle=0$. The $2^b$ vectors are therefore orthonormal.

*Lemma 2.* $\langle P_{y'}g_s|g^{(c)}_+\rangle=0$ unless $s=\epsilon(y',c):=(-1)^{y'\cdot(Ly'\oplus c)}$, and $\langle P_{y'}g_s|g^{(c)}_-\rangle=0$ unless $s=-\epsilon(y',c)$. Using $X^{\tilde y}Z^{(0,c)}=(-1)^{y'\cdot c}Z^{(0,c)}X^{\tilde y}$, $\langle P_{y'}g_s|Q_cg_+\rangle=(-1)^{y'\cdot c}\langle g_s|Z^{(0,k)}X^{\tilde y}|g_+\rangle$ with $k=Ly'\oplus c$. Since $|\tilde y|$ is odd, $Z^{(0,k)}X^{\tilde y}$ exchanges the eigenspaces of $G$ and only the cross terms survive: the overlap is $(-1)^{y'\cdot c}\tfrac12A[1+s(-1)^{y'\cdot k}]$ with $A=\langle u|Z^{(0,k)}X^{\tilde y}|v\rangle$, where we used $\langle v|Z^{(0,k)}X^{\tilde y}|u\rangle=\langle u|X^{\tilde y}Z^{(0,k)}|v\rangle=(-1)^{y'\cdot k}A$ for real vectors. This vanishes unless $s=(-1)^{y'\cdot k}$. For $g^{(c)}_-=Gg^{(c)}_+$, the relations $GX^{\tilde y}=-X^{\tilde y}G$, $[G,Z^{(0,t)}]=0$ and $Gg_s=g_{-s}$ give $\langle P_{y'}g_s|g^{(c)}_-\rangle=-\langle P_{y'}g_{-s}|g^{(c)}_+\rangle$, which vanishes unless $s=-\epsilon(y',c)$.

With $E_c=\sum_{y'}|P_{y'}g_{\epsilon(y',c)}\rangle\langle P_{y'}g_{\epsilon(y',c)}|$, Lemma 2 and the completeness of the basis give $E_cg^{(c)}_+=g^{(c)}_+$ and $E_cg^{(c)}_-=0$, which proves the theorem. The same construction applies to the all-flipped blocks with $\varphi$ in place of $\psi$, and to the six-state probes once the bases are announced.

## Appendix B. Reduction to collective attacks under the timelock

The key length of Section 4 is evaluated for the symmetric collective attack. Three steps relate it to general attacks. (1) Tagging. As in Ref. [T25], rounds in which Alice emits more than one photon or Bob's detector does not fire are tagged and the adversary is credited with full knowledge of them; the decoy-state bounds $S^-_{\bar K}$ and $\phi^+_Z$ are statements about Bob's click record and hold against any attack. The untagged rounds are single-photon rounds, so after squashing the single-round system is a pair of qubits, $d=4$. (2) Collective to coherent. For a permutation-invariant protocol, the post-selection technique [CKR09] states that security with parameter $\epsilon$ against every i.i.d. attack $\sigma^{\otimes n}$ implies security with parameter $\epsilon(n+1)^{d^2-1}$ against arbitrary attacks, because a purification of any permutation-invariant state is obtained from a purification of the de Finetti state $\tau_n=\int\sigma^{\otimes n}\,d\sigma$ by a completely positive map on the purifying system that succeeds with probability at least $(n+1)^{-(d^2-1)}$. Under the timelock the adversary applies a measurement to her purification before the delayed messages are sent; the composition of the post-selection map with that measurement is again a measurement applied before the messages, so a timelock-restricted distinguisher for a coherent attack yields a timelock-restricted distinguisher for the de Finetti state with the stated loss. The i.i.d. statement must therefore cover every $\sigma$. (3) Symmetrisation. Alice and Bob may apply a random Pauli twirl to the untagged rounds and announce it at once; it commutes with their measurements, costs no key and reduces any collective attack to a Bell-diagonal one with the observed error rates $\phi_X,\phi_Z$ [KGR05]. The symmetric attack of Section 3 is the Bell-diagonal attack with $\phi_X=\phi_Z=D$ and the one free parameter $c_\varphi$. A Bell-diagonal attack with $\phi_X\neq\phi_Z$ has a two-parameter purification over which the adversary term would have to be re-maximised; we have not done so, which is why the finite-key numbers of Section 5 are a model calculation within the symmetric family. The negative conclusion needs only one attack in the family and is unaffected.
